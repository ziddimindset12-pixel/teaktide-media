How to Import from India to the USA: A Plain-English Guide (2026)

Suppliers, paperwork, duties, shipping and payment, explained without the jargon.

9 MIN READ · IMPORT GUIDE · By Ashu Dhakre

Importing from India to the US isn't hard. It's just unfamiliar. Most first-time buyers worry about the wrong things (the paperwork) and not enough about the things that actually cost them money (the wrong supplier, an unclear spec, a container that arrives wet).

This guide walks through the whole thing in the order it really happens. No legal advice here, and your customs broker gets the final word on your specific product, but by the end you'll know what to ask and who does what.

Step 1: Get clear on what you're actually buying

Before you contact a single supplier, write down the product the way a factory would read it. Not "cotton bedsheets" but "300 thread count, 100% cotton percale, queen size, white, 4-piece set, retail polybag with insert card, 1,500 sets." Indian suppliers are quick to quote. If your brief is vague, you'll get five quotes for five different products and no way to compare them.

Add your target price, the trade term you want (FOB Indian port or delivered to your US door), and when you need the goods. That one page saves weeks of back-and-forth.

Step 2: Find the supplier in the right place

India is big, and most products come from a specific cluster. Brass and metal decor comes from Moradabad. Knitwear comes from Tiruppur. Home textiles come from Panipat and Karur. Leather bags come from Kanpur, Agra, Kolkata and Chennai. Black pepper and cardamom come from Kerala. Buying from a factory inside the cluster usually means better price and fewer middlemen.

B2B portals are a fine starting point, but plenty of listings are traders, not makers. Ask for the factory address, the IEC (Import Export Code) and GST number, and a video call walking the production floor. A genuine manufacturer won't mind. If you can't visit, a local sourcing agent or a third-party inspector can do it for you.

Step 3: Classify the product (HTS code)

Every product entering the US has a 10-digit Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) code. The code decides your duty rate and whether any other agency, like the FDA, needs to look at the shipment. You can search the schedule yourself at hts.usitc.gov, but have your customs broker confirm it. A wrong code is one of the most common reasons for surprise duty bills or delays.

Step 4: Understand what you'll pay at the border

Your landed cost has a few layers:

  • Regular duty: the normal rate for your HTS code. For many handicrafts it's low or zero; for apparel and textiles it's often 10% to 30%.
  • Extra tariffs on Indian goods: these have changed several times since 2025. At the time of writing (November 2026), most Indian goods face an additional 10% duty on top of the normal rate. It can change again, so check the current figure with your broker before you finalize pricing.
  • Small fees: the Merchandise Processing Fee and, for sea freight, the Harbor Maintenance Fee. Small percentages, but they add up on big orders.

One more thing: the old $800 "de minimis" rule that let small parcels in duty-free is suspended. Even small sample shipments by courier can now attract duty, so budget for it.

Step 5: Set up the importer side

You (or your company) will be the Importer of Record. In practice that means:

  • An EIN or a CBP-assigned importer number.
  • A licensed customs broker who files your entry. Most first-time importers use one, and it's money well spent.
  • A customs bond. Commercial shipments over $2,500 need one. If you'll import more than a few times a year, a continuous bond is usually cheaper than buying a single-entry bond each time.
  • For ocean freight, an ISF (Importer Security Filing, the "10+2") filed at least 24 hours before the container is loaded in India. Late or missing ISF filings can be fined, so get your broker and your Indian supplier talking early.

Step 6: Check if another agency is involved

This is where a lot of first shipments get stuck.

  • Food, spices, herbs, supplements: the Indian facility must be registered with the FDA, a Prior Notice must be filed before arrival, and you as importer are responsible under the Foreign Supplier Verification Program (FSVP). Spices are tested for things like salmonella, so a pre-shipment lab test is cheap insurance.
  • Kids' products and many consumer goods: CPSC rules apply. Children's products need a Children's Product Certificate based on testing at an accepted lab.
  • Wood and plant-based items (wooden decor, furniture, bamboo, some baskets): the Lacey Act may require a declaration of the species and where it was harvested.
  • Textiles and apparel: labels must show fiber content, country of origin and who's responsible for the product (often an RN number).

Everything also has to be marked with its country of origin, so "Made in India" on the product or packaging, as your category requires.

Step 7: Agree on trade terms and payment

FOB (the supplier delivers to the Indian port, you handle freight from there) gives you control over shipping cost. CIF means the supplier books freight to your port, which is easier but often hides a margin. DDP (delivered duty paid) is rare from Indian manufacturers and usually priced high.

The most common payment terms are 30% advance and 70% against a copy of the bill of lading, or 30% advance and 70% before dispatch after inspection. For larger orders, a Letter of Credit protects both sides. Avoid paying 100% upfront to a supplier you haven't verified, however good the price looks.

Step 8: Approve samples, then inspect before shipping

Approve a pre-production sample, keep one signed copy each, and write the tolerances down. Then book a pre-shipment inspection when production is at least 80% packed. An inspector checks quantity, workmanship, measurements, labels and packing against your order, usually using AQL sampling. It costs a few hundred dollars. Finding the problem in New Jersey costs a lot more.

Step 9: Shipping times and ports

Most sea freight leaves from Nhava Sheva (Mumbai), Mundra, Chennai or Kolkata. Rough port-to-port transit times to the US are about 30 to 45 days to either coast, depending on the route and how many times the container is transshipped, plus a few days on each end for customs and trucking. Air freight takes about 3 to 7 days door to door but is priced by weight and volume, so it only makes sense for samples, urgent orders or light, high-value goods.

For small orders, LCL (less than container load) lets you share a container. Once you're near 15 cubic meters, a full 20-foot container often works out cheaper per unit.

A quick checklist

  • Written spec, target price and trade term
  • Supplier verified: factory, IEC, GST, video walk-through
  • HTS code confirmed by your broker
  • Landed cost worked out, including the current tariff on Indian goods
  • Customs bond and ISF arranged before loading
  • FDA, CPSC, Lacey Act or labeling rules checked for your product
  • Sample approved, inspection booked, balance paid only after inspection

Do these nine things in order and your first container from India will be boring, which is exactly what you want.
